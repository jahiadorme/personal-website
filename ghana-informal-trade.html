Ghana’s GH₵7.4 Billion Informal Trade: A Mirror to Industrial Policy Failures and Ghana’s Strategic Pivot for AfCFTA Readiness

Introduction

Newly released data from the Ghana Statistical Service (GSS) inaugural Informal Cross-Border Trade (ICBT) survey for Q4 2024 reveal a parallel economy of striking magnitude:

GH¢7.4 billion in unrecorded trade with Togo, Burkina Faso, and Côte d’Ivoire—equivalent to 4.3% of Ghana’s total trade.

  • Informal exports: GH¢3.9 billion (4.1% of total exports)
  • Informal imports: GH¢3.5 billion (4.7% of total imports)

Regional Trade Dominance

Informal trade dominates Ghana's total commerce with:

  • Togo: 61.2%
  • Côte d’Ivoire: 55.7%

These are not marginal flows—they represent a structural feature of Ghana’s regional economy, driven predominantly by micro-entrepreneurs and small-scale transporters.

Dual Policy Challenge

The data exposes two critical policy failures:

  1. Industrial and agro-processing policy ambitions have not translated into competitive domestic production.
  2. Fiscal leakage undermines the Medium-Term Revenue Strategy (MTRS) and foreign-exchange reserves.

Commodity Composition

Top Informal Exports

  • Beverages: GH¢357 million
  • Livestock: GH¢177 million
  • Petroleum products: GH¢113 million

Top Informal Imports

  • Cooking oil: GH¢271 million
  • Rice: GH¢143 million
  • Livestock: GH¢306 million
  • Mattresses: GH¢171 million

This pattern signals reverse industrialization: Ghana is informally importing goods that industrial policies aimed to produce locally.

The recorded trade deficit with Togo alone (approximately GH¢539 million in informal channels) quantifies the cost of this gap in productive capacity.

Fiscal and Foreign-Exchange Implications

Every cedi spent on informally imported goods bypasses:

  • VAT
  • Customs duties
  • Corporate taxes

This leakage worsens foreign-exchange pressures and debt-service burdens.
Gender dynamics: Men dominate export transport (65.7%), while women account for 41.3% of import transporters—especially in northern and western borders.

Strategic Policy Imperatives

The GSS data shifts the conversation from whether to act to how to act intelligently. Four coordinated interventions are non-negotiable:

Strategic Focus Why It Matters Recommended Action
Fiscal Formalisation GH¢7.4 bn outside tax net; micro-traders dominate Introduce tiered tax registration, flexible payment schedules, and trader ID system with incentives
Targeted Industrial Support Imports include policy-targeted goods Allocate Ghana EXIM Bank funds to agro-industrial production; link 24-hour economy factories to border markets
Border Infrastructure & Trade Facilitation Corridors rely on tricycles and head porterage Fast-track ECOWAS Trade Facilitation Project; create trader lanes and low-cost warehousing
AfCFTA-Ready Regional Cooperation Informal trade dominates regional exchanges Harmonise customs procedures; share real-time trade data; pilot joint border markets

From Leakage to Leverage

Informal trade is not a nuisance—it reflects Ghana’s structural weaknesses. The policy response must be:

  • Integrated
  • Forward-looking
  • AfCFTA-aligned

Turning this GH¢7.4 billion shadow economy into a formal growth engine is not optional—it is imperative.
