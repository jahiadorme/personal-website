Ghana’s GH¢7.4 Billion Informal Cross-Border Trade: A Mirror to Industrial Policy Failures and Ghana’s Strategic Pivot for AfCFTA Readiness

Newly released data from the Ghana Statistical Service’s (GSS) inaugural Informal Cross-Border Trade (ICBT) survey for Q4 2024 reveal a parallel economy of striking magnitude: **GH¢7.4 billion** in unrecorded trade with Togo, Burkina Faso, and Côte d’Ivoire, equivalent to 4.3% of Ghana’s total trade. Informal exports reached GH¢3.9 billion (4.1% of total exports), while informal imports stood at GH¢3.5 billion (4.7% of total imports).

Informal trade dominates Ghana's total commerce with Togo (61.2%) and Côte d’Ivoire (55.7%).

These are not marginal flows. They represent a structural feature of Ghana’s regional economy, driven predominantly by micro-entrepreneurs and small-scale transporters. The data exposes a dual policy challenge:

1. A persistent failure to translate industrial and agro-processing policy ambitions into competitive domestic production.

2. A significant fiscal leakage that undermines the Medium-Term Revenue Strategy (MTRS) and foreign-exchange reserves.

What the Commodity Composition Tells Us

The top informal exports, beverages (GH¢357 million), livestock (GH¢177 million), and petroleum products (GH¢113 million), demonstrate pockets of domestic competitiveness, particularly in agro-processing and light manufacturing. By contrast, the leading informal imports, cooking oil (GH¢271 million), rice (GH¢143 million), livestock (GH¢306 million), and mattresses (GH¢171 million), are goods that successive industrial policies have explicitly targeted for local production and import substitution.

This pattern is not accidental. It is empirical evidence of deindustrialisation in reverse: Ghana has become a net importer, through informal channels, of precisely the manufactured and processed agricultural goods that policies such as the 24 hour economy initiative and the Ghana Industrial Policy were designed to produce domestically. The recorded trade deficit with Togo alone (approximately GH¢539 million in informal channels) quantifies the cost of this gap in productive capacity.

Fiscal and Foreign-Exchange Implications

Every cedi spent on informally imported cooking oil, rice, or mattresses exits the formal financial system, bypassing VAT, customs duties, and corporate taxes. In an economy facing chronic foreign-exchange pressures and a heavy debt-service burden, this leakage is not merely a statistical curiosity, it is a direct drag on macroeconomic stability.

Moreover, the gendered nature of the trade demands attention: while men dominate export transport (65.7%), women account for 41.3% of import transporters, particularly in northern and western border regions. Formalisation strategies that ignore this reality risk deepening gender inequalities in access to finance, markets, and social protection.

From Leakage to Leverage

Informal trade is not a nuisance—it is a mirror reflecting Ghana’s structural weaknesses. The policy response must be integrated and forward-looking, combining fiscal formalization, industrial revitalization, and regional cooperation under AfCFTA. Turning this GH₵7.4 billion shadow economy into a formal growth engine is not optional,it is imperative.
